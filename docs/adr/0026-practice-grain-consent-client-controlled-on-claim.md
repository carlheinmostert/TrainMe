# Consent is practice-grain; practitioner-proxy pre-claim, client-controlled on claim

The consent matrix lives at **practice grain** — one consent record per practice a client is linked to, not per-practitioner and not global across the consumer's whole life. Six toggles: `line_drawing` (always-true, locked — the conversion pipeline de-identifies and the result can't be withdrawn; see [ADR-0014](./0014-three-treatment-storage-split.md) and the `clients.video_consent` jsonb shape in [`supabase/archive/schema_milestone_g_three_treatment.sql`](../../supabase/archive/schema_milestone_g_three_treatment.sql)), `grayscale` (B&W), `original` (full-colour), `avatar` (profile photo display), `face_recognition` (the on-device MobileFaceNet biometric embedding introduced by the Safe Mode v2 face-recognition discriminator — see [`docs/CHECKPOINT_2026-05-24.md`](../CHECKPOINT_2026-05-24.md) and [`docs/CHECKPOINT_2026-05-25.md`](../CHECKPOINT_2026-05-25.md) — used to tell the client apart from bystanders during capture), and `analytics_allowed` (the Wave-17 plan-event surface). **Pre-claim**, the practitioner is the proxy: they set `clients.video_consent` on the practice's client row exactly as they do today, on the client's verbal in-person say-so. **Post-claim**, the consumer owns the matrix outright — full autonomy, no clinical carve-out, no practitioner override — *with transparency* to the practitioner who can see "analytics off for this client" surfaced in the consent panel but cannot flip the bits or fake them. On claim, the consumer's initial settings **inherit** the practitioner's current proxy state (continuity — nothing visibly changes between session N and session N+1); the consumer adjusts from there at their own pace. The claimed consumer account is a **spanning identity**: a single `auth.users` row that sits above the multiple practice client-rows it has linked, threading them together for the consumer's own purposes (one inbox, one plan history); consent records live on the consumer account at **consumer × practice** grain and override the practice's `clients.video_consent` for every read once the override exists. Every plan belongs to exactly one practice (`plans.practice_id` is non-null on every published row — see the tenancy boundary established by [ADR-0001](./0001-practice-as-tenancy-boundary.md) and the `practices` + `practice_members` model), so each artifact read resolves through the matching relationship's consent record. Per-relationship consent and per-plan gating are therefore the same query — no special cases, no global flags, no priority arbitration between competing records. See the consumer-consent panel mockup at `docs/design/mockups/2026-05-26-consumer-consent-panel.html` and the full design in `docs/ARTIFACT_SYSTEM.md` (Consent & POPIA + Identity & claiming sections, plus locked decisions #16 and #17).

## Considered Options

We considered **per-practitioner grain** — one consent record per individual practitioner the consumer works with, not per practice. Rejected because practitioners within a practice **share the client record by design**: clients exist at practice tenancy, not per-practitioner, and that boundary is load-bearing for the whole multi-tenant model. Splitting consent finer than the row it sits on creates a coherence problem with no clean answer: if Margaret and Helen both work at Cape Biokinetics and the client has different consent for each, which consent does a plan published by Margaret with Helen's pre-publish review read? Which one governs a session where Helen covers Margaret's slot? Practice-grain side-steps the question — the practice is the unit of trust the client agreed to, and every practitioner in it inherits the same access boundary. We considered **global per-consumer consent** — one record across all practices the consumer has linked, applied uniformly. Rejected because the consumer's openness varies legitimately by relationship: a client might be relaxed with their long-time biokineticist Margaret (avatar + analytics + face-rec all on) and strict with a gym trainer James they just started seeing two weeks ago (line-drawing only). Forcing the same level across all relationships drives consent to the lowest common denominator — the strictest setting any one relationship needs — which under-represents the real preference in every other relationship. Per-relationship grain is the only shape that lets the consumer say "yes here, no there" honestly. We considered **resetting consent to a safe default on claim** — start the consumer from zero (everything off except the locked line-drawing) instead of inheriting the practitioner's proxy state. Rejected because the practitioner has already shown the client the existing plans in person and the client has agreed in-person to what's there; flipping consent to off on claim silently changes what the practitioner's existing plans render as (B&W and Original would vanish, leaving line-drawings only), which is confusing, breaks trust both ways, and makes the claim moment feel adversarial. Inherit-on-claim with an explicit first-visit reading of "here's what's currently shared with this practice — you can change any of it" is the honest move: nothing visibly changes, the consumer is informed, and the next change is theirs. We considered a **clinical carve-out** — let the practitioner override the consumer's consent for stated clinical, safety, or treatment reasons. Rejected. The line-drawing pipeline already de-identifies enough for clinical observation of form and movement; the practitioner does not need stronger identity-data access to deliver good clinical care. Carve-outs of this kind erode the consumer's confidence that the toggles mean what they say — once "the practitioner can override for clinical reasons" exists, every toggle reads as soft. Transparency (the practitioner *sees* the consumer's current state) is the substitute that preserves clinical observation without giving the practitioner a tool to defeat consent. We considered **hiding the override from the practitioner entirely** — silent opt-out, the practitioner sees no signal that analytics or face-rec is off for client X. Rejected because it breaks the practitioner's mental model in a way that costs them clinical signal: if a client's session-engagement count looks dead, the practitioner needs to know whether that's a real disengagement worth following up on or a consent state that explains the silence. Hiding the override reduces consent to a black box; the practitioner ends up reading absent data as absent client, which damages the relationship the consumer is trying to preserve. Showing the override state without exposing the change history (no "client turned this off on 2026-05-26" audit chip in the practitioner UI) is the balance: the practitioner has enough signal to interpret what they're seeing, the consumer has full control of the bits themselves. The trade-off acknowledged in choosing transparency-not-override: some practitioners will dislike that they can see "analytics off" without being able to act on it. They are paying for the platform and may feel they should be able to override "for clinical reasons" — Carl's call is that consumer autonomy is the load-bearing trust principle and is not negotiable; practitioners who can't accept the asymmetry are not a fit for the platform we are building. A second trade-off: the spanning consumer identity introduces a new auth model (the consumer is a separate `auth.users` row from the practitioner's clients, addressable across practices), with schema implications spelled out in the design doc's open question on the exact `client_accounts` link-table shape — TBD on the shape, fixed on the grain (consumer × practice). A third trade-off: the pre-claim → post-claim flip means consent ownership *moves* mid-flight, which requires an atomic transfer of authority at claim time — consumer claims the client row, the consumer-side consent record is created with the inherited values from the proxy, and every subsequent read prefers the consumer-side record over the practice's `clients.video_consent`. This handoff is the only moment in the lifecycle where two consent records exist for the same logical relationship; after it, the consumer-side record is canonical and the proxy row becomes a frozen historical fact.
